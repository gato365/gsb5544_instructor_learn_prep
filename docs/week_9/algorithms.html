Cross-validation in motion K-fold on the same eight houses · regression · error = RMSE in $1,000s


Training error only says how well a model memorised the houses it was fitted on. To estimate how it will do on new houses, hold some houses out, fit on the rest, and measure the error on the held-out ones. K-fold cross-validation does that K times, so every house is held out exactly once: cut the data into K folds; for each fold, fit a fresh model on the other K − 1 folds and compute the error on this one; then average the K validation errors. The same folds are used for every candidate model, which is what makes the averages comparable.


Two different k's. k in k-nearest neighbors is how many training houses are averaged for one prediction (n_neighbors, a model setting). K in K-fold cross-validation is how many pieces the data is cut into (n_splits, a setting of the evaluation). This page writes the folds one as a capital K.

What every number on this page means


Grouped by where the number appears. "Learned" numbers come out of .fit(); "chosen" numbers (hyperparameters) are set by you before fitting.


Linear regression


intercept_ (33.29)
Learned. The line's predicted price, in $1,000s, at size 0. Mostly a positioning constant here; a size-0 house does not exist.


coef_ / slope (11.72)
Learned. Predicted change in price ($1,000s) per extra unit of size (100 sq ft): about $117 per square foot.


predicted
intercept + slope × size for a training house; the point on the line directly above or below it.


residual
actual price − predicted price, in $1,000s. Positive: the house sold for more than the line says; negative: for less. Zero would mean the point sits on the line.


sum of squared residuals (SSE, 715.1)
Add every residual squared. Least squares picks the intercept and slope that make this as small as possible, so no other line scores lower. Units are $1,000s squared, so compare it only between lines on the same data.


predict (297.0)
The line evaluated at the new house's size: model.predict(new).


K-nearest neighbors


k = n_neighbors
Chosen. How many training houses are averaged for one prediction. Small k follows the data closely (k = 1 copies the nearest house; the staircase is jagged); large k smooths (k = 8 is flat at the overall mean, 266.25). Training error rises with k; validation error usually falls then rises, and here (nearly linear data) simply rises.


distance
|training size − new size|, in 100s of sq ft. With one feature no scaling is needed; with several, scale first so one feature's units do not dominate.


neighbor? (✓)
Whether the house is among the k smallest distances. Ties for the last place are broken in favour of the earlier row, as scikit-learn does.


prediction
The mean price of the k neighbors; the staircase is this mean evaluated at every size.


Decision tree


candidate threshold t
A midpoint between two neighbouring training sizes; the question "size ≤ t?" splits the houses into a left (≤) and right (>) group. scikit-learn tries every midpoint.


SSE of a split
Left SSE + right SSE, where each side's SSE is the sum of squared gaps between its prices and its own mean. Smaller is better; the smallest candidate becomes the node's question.


samples
How many training houses reach this node. Root: 8. The two children of a node share its samples.


value
The mean price of the node's houses. For a leaf this is the prediction for every house that lands there.


squared_error
The node's mean squared error around its value: SSE ÷ samples. This is the "impurity" plot_tree prints. Zero means every house in the node has the same price.


max_depth
Chosen. The longest chain of questions allowed. Depth 1 = one question, two leaves; each extra level can double the leaves. Deeper → smaller steps, lower training error, and past some depth higher validation error (overfitting). The tree stops early when a node has one house or all equal prices.


leaves
Nodes with no question. The regression function has one flat value per leaf.


Cross-validation


K = n_splits
Chosen. Number of folds; each fold is the validation set exactly once, so the model is fitted K times. K = 8 here is leave-one-out (one house per fold). More folds → larger training sets (closer to the final model) but more fits and more variable estimates. 5 or 10 are the usual choices on real data.


shuffle, random_state
KFold cuts consecutive rows by default. The eight houses are sorted by size, so unshuffled folds hold out the smallest or largest houses together and the model must extrapolate: errors balloon. shuffle=True mixes the rows first; random_state fixes the mix so results repeat.


training RMSE (per fold)
√(mean of squared residuals) on the houses the fold's model was fitted on. Optimistic: the model has seen these prices.


validation RMSE (per fold)
√(mean of squared residuals) on the held-out houses, in $1,000s. The honest number for that fold: an estimate of the error on houses the model has not seen.


mean validation RMSE
Average over the K folds: the cross-validation estimate of out-of-sample error. Compare models and settings with this. The spread of the per-fold values shows how noisy the estimate is.


negative scores
cross_val_score(..., scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error") returns −RMSE per fold because scikit-learn treats every score as "higher is better". Negate (and average) to get the numbers in the table.

Preprocessing goes in a pipeline (next box) so that .fit() and .predict() stay the same two calls — and so that cross-validation refits the preprocessing inside every fold.






Definition Pipeline


A scikit-learn Pipeline combines preprocessing steps and a model into one ordered workflow that behaves like a single model: it has .fit() and .predict(), and that is all you call.


    

  1. pipe.fit(X_train, y_train) runs the steps in order: each preprocessing step learns what it needs from the training data (the mean and standard deviation for a scaler, the list of categories for a one-hot encoder), transforms the data, and passes it on; the model at the end is fitted on the transformed data.
    2. 

  2. pipe.predict(X_new) applies the same fitted preprocessing to the new observations — the training mean and standard deviation, the training categories, nothing is re-learned from the new rows — and then asks the model.
    3. 



A pipeline is not a prediction algorithm. It makes no predictions of its own; it organizes the steps so the right preprocessing always travels with the model, which is what makes "fit on training data, predict on new data" safe.


Why it matters for cross-validation. If you scale all the rows first and cross-validate afterwards, every fold's training step has already seen the validation rows' mean and standard deviation: a small data leak. Hand cross_val_score the pipeline instead, and the scaler is refitted inside each fold on the training rows only, exactly as it will be when real new data arrives.
