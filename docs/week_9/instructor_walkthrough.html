    Before you start

    The two k's

    The one confusion to pre-empt. Write both on the board before cross-validation starts.

    • k in k-nearest neighbors = n_neighbors: how many training houses are averaged for one prediction. A setting of the model.
    • K in K-fold cross-validation = n_splits: how many pieces the data is cut into. A setting of the evaluation.
    Say it aloud"We can cross-validate a 3-nearest-neighbor model with 4 folds. Little k is about the model, big K is about how we test it. They have nothing to do with each other."

    The page and the notebook write the folds one as a capital K throughout.

    1 · Linear regression

    What the model is doing

    The model is a line: price ≈ intercept + slope × size. .fit(X, y) searches over every intercept and slope and keeps the pair with the smallest sum of squared residuals. .predict(new) reads the new size off the line.

    On the page: three grey candidate lines appear, then the red winner with its equation, then the eight purple residuals one by one, then the orange new house rises to the line and across to the price axis.

    Say it aloud"Fitting means choosing. Out of every possible line, least squares picks the one whose squared vertical gaps add up to the least."

    1 · Linear regression

    Check and misconceptions

    Check: "Could a cleverer line get an SSE below 715.1?"

    No. 715.1 is the minimum by construction: least squares already searched every intercept and slope. A different line could have a smaller residual for one house, never a smaller sum of squares.

    • "The residuals tell us how the model does on new houses." They are training errors: the line was chosen to make exactly these gaps small. The honest number for new houses comes in section 5.
    • "A negative residual means the model is wrong." Every point off the line has a non-zero residual. Negative only says the house sold for less than predicted.

    2 · K-nearest neighbors

    What the model is doing

    Nothing is fitted. .fit(X, y) stores the eight rows. .predict(new) measures the distance from the new size to every training size, keeps the k closest, and averages their prices.

    On the page: green brackets along the bottom measure the distances one at a time (grey once past k), rings mark the k neighbors, the prediction appears, then the red staircase sweeps across.

    Say it aloud"KNN answers a question by asking the most similar houses what they sold for and averaging. With one feature, similar means close in size."

    2 · K-nearest neighbors

    Check and misconceptions

    Check: "With k = 8 the prediction is 266.25 for every size. Why, and is that a bug?"

    With 8 houses, every size has the same 8 neighbors, so every prediction is the overall mean. Not a bug: it is the most-smoothed model possible, and it ignores the feature completely. (PA 9.1 asks the same about 27-NN on the 27 wines: flat at 3.158.)

    • "KNN learns something during fit." It stores rows. All the work happens at prediction time, which is also why it is slow on big data.
    • "Nearest means closest in price." Distance is measured in the features (size). The prices of the neighbors are what gets averaged.
    • "Bigger k is always safer." k = 8 here ignores size; it is as wrong in its way as k = 1.
    • "k = 3 means three folds." No: see the two k's card.

    3 · Decision tree

    What the model is doing

    A tree asks yes/no questions about size. Fitting = finding the best question: for every candidate threshold, split the houses into a left group (≤) and a right group (>), score the split by its SSE (each house's squared gap to its own group's mean, both groups added), keep the smallest. Then repeat inside each group until max_depth.

    Predicting = answering the questions for the new house down to a leaf and reporting that leaf's mean price.

    Say it aloud"The tree is looking for the one cut that makes the two sides as internally similar in price as possible. Then it does it again on each side."
    Open on the site (max_depth = 2)

    3 · Decision tree

    Check and misconceptions

    Check: "A node reads samples = 5, value = 208, squared_error = 1,736. What is its SSE?"

    1,736 × 5 = 8,680. (This is the root's left child; its SSE is the "left" half of the 12,747.)

    Check: "Depth 3 has training RMSE 5 and depth 2 has 16.3. So depth 3 is better?"

    Not on that evidence. Training RMSE must fall with depth; it rewards memorizing. Section 7's cross-validation shows depth 2 predicting held-out houses better (31.9 vs 36.2).

    • "Thresholds sit on data points." They are midpoints between neighboring training values; any cut between two houses gives the same split, and scikit-learn uses the middle.
    • "squared_error is the error on new houses." It is the within-node spread of training prices: how unsure the leaf is about its own houses.
    • "A deeper tree knows more." It has memorized more. Knowing is measured on houses it has not seen.

    4 · Training, validation, test

    Three sets, three jobs

    setjobwho sees the prices
    trainingfit the model: .fit(X_train, y_train)the model, while fitting
    validationchoose between models and settingsyou, repeatedly, while deciding
    testone final honest report of the chosen model's errornobody, until the end; then once

    The rule that makes it work: the test set stays sealed while any choice is still open. Comparing twenty models on the test set and picking the best uses up the test set: its error is now an optimistic, chosen number.

    4 · Training, validation, test

    How to explain it aloud

    Say it aloud"Training data is the homework with the answers in the back: the model learns from it. Validation data is the practice exam you take to decide how to study: you can take it many times, and you use the results to make choices. The test set is the final exam: you sit it once, after every decision is made, and the grade is the grade."

    Then the twist students need: validation data is carved out of the training data. It is not a third delivery of data from somewhere; it is training rows you choose not to fit on, so they can stand in for unseen rows. Cross-validation (section 6) is just a disciplined way of doing that carving several times.

    4 · Training, validation, test

    The pre-class reading's data

    The reading (Estimating the Test Error) uses the Bordeaux wines: 38 vintages, but prices are known only for the 27 vintages up to 1980. Target: log(price). Features: winter rain (win) and summer temperature (summer).

    • The 27 known vintages are the training data.
    • The 11 later vintages are test data in the truest sense: their prices are missing, so no test error can ever be computed. Prediction is blind.
    • So the reading carves a validation half out of the 27 (it used .sample(frac=0.5); the notebook uses train_test_split(test_size=0.5, random_state=5544): 13 training, 14 validation).

    The notebook's section 3 reproduces this; its section 7 switches to the Advertising data (200 markets) because there a real test set with known answers exists.

    4 · Training, validation, test

    Check and misconceptions

    Check: "I tried k = 1…20 and reported the test RMSE of the best one. What is wrong?"

    The test set made the choice, so its error is no longer an honest estimate: with twenty tries the smallest test error is partly luck, and nothing is left to report. Choose on validation data (or cross-validation), then test once.

    Check: "After choosing k on the validation set, which rows do I fit the final model on?"

    All the rows with known answers (training + validation). The split was a device for choosing; the final model should use everything. The reading's "important note" says exactly this.

    • "Validation and test are the same thing." Validation is used many times to decide; test is used once to report.
    • "We need more data to have a validation set." It comes out of the training rows you already have.
    • "scikit-learn's test_score is on the test set." In cross_validate it is the held-out fold: our validation score.

    5 · Training vs validation error

    Same formula, different rows

    RMSE = √(mean of squared errors), where error = actual − predicted. It is in the target's units, so you can say "off by about 9 thousand dollars".

    • Training RMSE: computed on the rows the model was fitted on. Optimistic: the model has seen these answers.
    • Validation RMSE: computed on rows it was not fitted on. The honest estimate of error on new rows.

    Same function in both cases: root_mean_squared_error(actual, predicted). Only the rows change.

    5 · Training vs validation error

    Check and misconceptions

    Check: "Why is 1-NN training error exactly zero? Name another model from today that gets zero."

    Each training row's nearest neighbor is itself, distance 0, so it predicts its own price. A tree with max_depth=None (one house per leaf) also gets 0.

    • "Lower training error means a better model." It means a more flexible model. Flexibility memorizes.
    • "Validation error is always higher than training error." Usually, not always; tiny data flips it by chance.
    • "RMSE is a percentage." It is in the target's units ($1,000s, log price, thousands of units sold).

    6 · K-fold cross-validation

    The idea in four moves

    1. Partition the rows into K folds (shuffle first unless the row order is already random).
    2. For fold 1: hold it out, fit a fresh model on the other K − 1 folds, score it on fold 1.
    3. Repeat for every fold. Each row is held out exactly once; each model is scored only on rows it never saw.
    4. Summarize: average the K validation errors. The spread shows how noisy the estimate is.
    Say it aloud"One validation split wastes half the data and depends on luck. Cross-validation takes turns: every house gets to be the held-out house once, and we average."
    Open on the site (3-NN, K = 4)

    6 · K-fold cross-validation

    What to notice when you change the folds

    • Shuffle off, K = 4: folds are consecutive rows {1,2}, {3,4}, {5,6}, {7,8}. The rows are sorted by size, so folds 1 and 4 hold out the smallest or largest houses and the model must extrapolate: validation RMSEs 73.2, 8.5, 30.7, 111.2, mean 55.91. Shuffle off, K = 2: mean 127.24. The lesson: KFold does not shuffle unless told to, and that is a trap on sorted data.
    • K = 8 (leave-one-out): eight fits on seven houses each; 3-NN mean 39.17. Training sets are bigger, so each fitted model is closer to the final one, but the eight scores are highly correlated.
    • K = 2, shuffled: two fits on four houses; 3-NN mean 60.66. Little data per fit, so pessimistic.

    6 · K-fold cross-validation

    The code behind the animation

    kf = KFold(n_splits=4, shuffle=True, random_state=5544)
scores = []
for train_idx, val_idx in kf.split(X):           # one pass per fold
    model = KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=3)   # fresh model
    model.fit(X.iloc[train_idx], y.iloc[train_idx])
    pred = model.predict(X.iloc[val_idx])
    scores.append(root_mean_squared_error(y.iloc[val_idx], pred))
np.mean(scores)                                  # 49.07

cross_val_score(KNeighborsRegressor(3), X, y, cv=kf,
                scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error")
# array([-38.87, -17.95, -60.42, -79.06]) -> negate, average

    The page highlights each line as the matching step plays. Two things to say out loud: kf.split(X) yields row positions, hence .iloc; and the scores are negative because scikit-learn treats every score as higher-is-better, so an error is reported as its negative.

    6 · K-fold cross-validation

    Check and misconceptions

    Check: "5-fold CV of a 10-NN model on 150 rows: how many models, fitted on how many rows, scored on how many? Which is k and which is K?"

    Five models, each fitted on 120 rows and scored on 30; the five RMSEs are averaged. k = 10 neighbors (model), K = 5 folds (evaluation).

    Check: "cross_val_score returned [−0.9, −1.1, −0.8, −1.0, −1.2]. The estimate?"

    Negate and average: 1.0.

    • "Cross-validation gives us the model." It gives an error estimate. The final model is refitted on all the training rows afterwards.
    • "k folds = k neighbors." Two k's card.
    • "The folds overlap." Validation folds are disjoint: each row is held out once. Training sets overlap heavily (which is why many folds give correlated scores).
    • "The scores are errors." They are negative scores; negate them.
    • "cv=5 shuffles." It does not. Pass a KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=…) when the rows are ordered.

    7 · Comparing models and settings

    How to say the protocol aloud

    Say it aloud"Step one: split off a test set and put it in a sealed envelope. Step two: cross-validate every candidate on the training set, using the same folds for all of them. Step three: pick the smallest mean validation error. Step four: refit the winner on the whole training set, open the envelope, and report one test error. Then stop."

    If a student asks "why not just use cross-validation on all the data and skip the test set?": the reading says you may, if you only need to compare models. The test set is for a number you can quote to someone else as honest, after all your choosing is done.

    7 · Comparing models and settings

    Check and misconceptions

    Check: "The CV estimate for the tree was 0.93 and its test RMSE 1.40. Did cross-validation fail?"

    No. CV estimates the error from 30-market folds and 120-market fits; the 50 test markets are a different sample. The estimate was rough, the ranking was right. With more data both numbers settle down.

    • "Pick the model with the best test error." Pick on validation / CV; test once.
    • "The CV estimate is the test error." It is an estimate of it; expect them to differ.
    • "Different candidates can use different folds." Use the same kf for all, or the comparison mixes noise with signal.

    8 · Pipelines and leakage

    What a pipeline is

    A Pipeline chains preprocessing steps and a model into one object with one .fit() and one .predict(). On fit, each step learns from the training rows only (a scaler: mean and sd; a one-hot encoder: the categories), transforms, passes on; the model at the end is fitted on the result. On predict, the same fitted preprocessing is applied; nothing is re-learned from the new rows.

    A pipeline is not a prediction algorithm. It makes no predictions of its own. (The definition box at the bottom of the page is editable.)

    make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=5))

    8 · Pipelines and leakage

    Why it belongs in this lesson: leakage

    Cross-validation only tells the truth if each fold's model sees nothing from its validation rows. Scaling is fitting: StandardScaler().fit(X) learns a mean and sd from whatever rows it gets.

    • ❌ Scale all 27 wines, then cross_val_score: the scaler already saw every fold's validation rows. Mean CV RMSE 0.5308.
    • ✅ cross_val_score(pipeline, …): the whole pipeline is refitted inside each fold, scaler included. Mean CV RMSE 0.5372.
    Be ready for thisThe leak is small here, and the leaky number is even slightly better-looking. Say so. The point is procedural: the leaky version answers a question nobody asked. With imputation, feature selection or target encoding the leak can be large. GridSearchCV packages the section-7 loop and does the right thing automatically (it picked k = 2, test RMSE 0.81, same as by hand).

    8 · Pipelines and leakage

    Check and misconceptions

    Check: "A classmate scales the whole X, then cross-validates KNN. What is wrong and what is the fix?"

    The scaler saw the validation rows of every fold (their contribution to the mean and sd). Fix: hand cross_val_score the pipeline so the scaler is refitted on training rows inside each fold.

    • "A pipeline is a kind of model." It is a container; the last step is the model.
    • "Scaling before splitting is harmless because it is just a formula." The formula's constants come from the data. That is fitting.
    • "Trees need scaling too." Only distance-based models (KNN) do; all three need categoricals one-hot encoded.
