1 · The prediction problem

Predict next week's sales at a coffee shop you have never seen.

A regional chain has 100 locations. For each one we know ten things about the store and its neighborhood, and last week's sales. The chain is opening new stores and wants a sales forecast from the store's characteristics alone.

Simulated data Generated in your browser from a fixed random seed so every number on this page is reproducible. Real sales data would not come with a known answer key.

First 6 of 100 locations. 60 are used for training and cross-validation; 40 are locked away as a held-out test set until Section 4.

The ten predictors

    Some of these overlap (seats, parking, and store size all describe how big the store is). Some may have nothing to do with sales. That is the point.

    Answer key (only possible because the data are simulated). Weekly sales were built from foot traffic, ad spend, store size, neighborhood income, and competitor count, plus random noise of about $2,800. Social posts, seats, and parking are correlated copies of other predictors (correlation near 0.9) with no effect of their own. Temperature and manager tenure are pure noise.

    Keep this in mind in Section 3: a model that keeps "seats" and drops "store size" has not discovered anything about seats.

    2 · One slider, three things move

    Regularization: a penalty for large coefficients.

    Ordinary least squares picks coefficients that minimize training error, full stop. A regularized model minimizes training error plus a penalty that grows with the size of the coefficients. The slider sets how heavy that penalty is. Watch the coefficients, the two errors, and the predictions move together.

    RMSE is in dollars of weekly sales: "typical miss." Lower is better.

    Predicted vs. observed

    Too little penalty leaves the coefficients free to chase noise in these 60 rows. Too much drags every coefficient toward zero, including the useful ones, and predictions collapse toward the average. The CV curve has a low point in between; the training curve only ever gets worse as the penalty grows, which is why training error cannot pick the penalty.

    Why standardize first? The penalty treats a coefficient of 500 as "big" no matter what the variable is. But a coefficient on "store size in square feet" and one on "manager tenure in years" live on completely different scales. Standardizing each predictor (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation) puts every coefficient in the same units: dollars per one standard deviation. Then the penalty is fair, and the bars above are comparable.

    The equations, if you want them
    OLS: minimize Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²
    Ridge: minimize Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + alpha · Σ βⱼ²
    LASSO: minimize (1/2n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + alpha · Σ |βⱼ|

    The intercept is not penalized. scikit-learn's Ridge and Lasso scale the squared-error term differently (Lasso divides by 2n), so an alpha of 10 does not mean the same thing for both. That is why the slider's alpha range changes when you switch methods.

    3 · Two penalties, two personalities

    Ridge shrinks. LASSO shrinks and selects.

    Same data, same five folds. The only change is the shape of the penalty: squared coefficients (ridge) or absolute values (LASSO). Predict first, then look.

    Show paths for

    Coefficient paths: every predictor's coefficient as the penalty grows

    How to read a coefficient path

    Each line is one predictor. Left to right, the penalty gets heavier. At the far left the lines sit near their no-penalty (OLS) values. Moving right, every line heads toward zero. Under ridge they approach zero smoothly and never quite land. Under LASSO lines hit exactly zero and stay there; once a line is flat on the axis, that predictor is out of the model. The dashed vertical line marks the alpha that 5-fold cross-validation chose (Section 4 shows how).

    Side by side at each method's CV-chosen alpha

    • Ridge shrinks toward zero but almost never reaches it. Every predictor stays in the model; correlated predictors tend to share their effect.
    • LASSO can set coefficients to exactly zero. The result is a smaller model that is easier to explain and cheaper to collect data for. Among correlated predictors, LASSO often keeps one and drops the rest, somewhat arbitrarily.
    • Neither is automatically better. On this dataset the two CV errors are close. Ridge tends to win when many predictors each matter a little; LASSO when a few matter and the rest are noise. You find out by cross-validating, not by assuming.
    • Retained does not mean important, and certainly not causal. Check the answer key in Section 1: if LASSO kept "seats" and dropped "store size," it chose a stand-in, not a cause. Regularization is a prediction tool. It does not test hypotheses.
    4 · How the slider gets set in practice

    Grid search: try every candidate, let cross-validation vote.

    We cannot choose alpha by training error (it always prefers no penalty) and we must not choose it by test error (then the test set is no longer a fair test). So we split the training data into folds and let the folds judge each candidate.

    Where every row lives

    The 60 training rows are dealt into 5 folds. For each candidate alpha, each fold takes a turn as the validation set while the other four train the model. The 40 test rows take no part in this. They are opened once, at the very end.

    5 · A dial between the two

    Elastic net mixes the ridge and LASSO penalties.

    Two tuning parameters now: alpha for how heavy the total penalty is, and l1_ratio for how much of it is LASSO-style (absolute values) versus ridge-style (squares). Grid search now runs over a grid in two dimensions, which is what the heatmap shows.

    In scikit-learn, alpha is the overall penalty strength and l1_ratio is the mixture. l1_ratio = 1 is exactly the LASSO penalty; values between 0 and 1 blend the two. For the pure ridge comparison use Ridge directly rather than l1_ratio = 0 (sklearn's ElasticNet solver is not meant for that end).

    Correlated predictors are the reason elastic net exists. When store size, seats, and parking all say nearly the same thing, pure LASSO tends to pick one more or less at random and zero the others, and which one it picks can change if you change the sample. The ridge component spreads the shared effect across the group so the selection is steadier, while the LASSO component still removes predictors that contribute nothing. Move l1_ratio from 1.0 toward 0.1 and watch the overlapping trio in the bar chart.

    The equation
    ElasticNet: minimize (1/2n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + alpha · l1_ratio · Σ |βⱼ| + alpha · (1 − l1_ratio)/2 · Σ βⱼ²
    6 · From the dials to scikit-learn

    The same four ideas in Python.

    Every control on this page maps to one argument. Code is kept inside a Pipeline so that standardization is re-estimated inside each cross-validation fold; otherwise the validation rows leak into the scaler.

    Negative scoring, positive numbers. scikit-learn's model-selection tools maximize a score, so error metrics are passed as negatives: scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error" returns values like −3058. Larger (closer to zero) is better. Flip the sign before reporting and say "RMSE ≈ $3,058," which is what this page does.

    About the numbers. Every coefficient, prediction, and error on this page is computed live in your browser: ridge by its closed-form solution, LASSO and elastic net by coordinate descent on scikit-learn's objective functions, with 5-fold cross-validation on fixed fold assignments. Nothing is pre-drawn. The dataset is simulated from a fixed seed; the "Try a different simulated dataset" button (student mode) draws a new one and recomputes everything, so results will change. That variability is real and worth discussing.
